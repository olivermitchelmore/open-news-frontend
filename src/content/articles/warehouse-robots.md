The warehouse on the edge of the motorway is the size of eleven football pitches, and on a Tuesday afternoon it is humming. Squat orange robots glide across the floor carrying entire shelving units to picking stations, where people pull items into boxes.

Five years ago, the same building employed around 600 people. Today it employs 640.

"Everyone assumed the robots would replace us," said Sam, a shift leader who has worked here since before the machines arrived. "They replaced the walking. I used to do 20 kilometres a day. Now the shelves come to me."

## Different jobs, not fewer

Across the logistics sector, the story is similar. Automation has grown rapidly, but so has demand for online deliveries, and the number of warehouse jobs has kept rising. What has changed is the mix. There are fewer pickers and more technicians, maintenance engineers and people who manage the software that directs the robots.

Pay has risen for those who have retrained. The company offers a twelve-week course in robot maintenance, and graduates earn around 30 per cent more than they did on the picking line.

## The pressure point

Not everyone has benefited. Unions say the pace of work at picking stations is now set by algorithms, and some workers report that targets rise every time the system is updated. The company says its targets are reviewed by health and safety staff.

"The robots are fine," Sam said. "It's the maths behind them you have to keep an eye on."
