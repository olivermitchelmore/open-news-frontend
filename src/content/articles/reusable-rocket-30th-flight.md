A rocket booster landed upright on a drone ship in the Atlantic on Wednesday evening, completing its 30th flight and setting a new record for the reuse of an orbital launch vehicle.

The booster, which first flew four years ago, carried a batch of communications satellites into low Earth orbit before returning to land about nine minutes after lift-off. Engineers said it showed no signs of wear that would prevent further flights.

## Cheaper access to space

Reusability has transformed the economics of spaceflight. The cost of putting a kilogram into orbit has fallen by more than 90 per cent since the early 2010s, opening up space to universities, small companies and countries with modest budgets.

"When I started my career, you would build a satellite to last 15 years because the launch was so expensive," said Dr Chiara Bellini, a satellite engineer. "Now we build them for five, and replace them with better ones."

## The next step

The operator's stated aim is to refly a booster within 24 hours of landing, something that would make rockets operate more like aircraft. The current record for turnaround is just under nine days.

Astronomers continue to raise concerns about the growing number of satellites in orbit, which can leave streaks across telescope images and add to the risk of collisions. Regulators are consulting on new rules for how satellites are disposed of at the end of their lives.
