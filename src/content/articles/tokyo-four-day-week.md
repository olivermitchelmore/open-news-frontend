When Tokyo's metropolitan government offered its staff the option of a four-day working week, the stated aim was ambitious: to make it easier for parents to raise children in one of the most expensive cities in the world, and in doing so to nudge up a birth rate that has fallen for eight years in a row.

A year later, almost a quarter of eligible employees have taken up the offer. Staff surveys show large improvements in reported stress and time spent with family. Absence rates have fallen. What has not moved, at least not yet, is the number of babies.

## Too early to judge

Demographers caution against expecting quick results. "Fertility decisions are made over years, not months," said Professor Yui Morimoto, a demographer in Tokyo. "And a single employer, however large, is a small part of a city's labour market."

The more interesting shift may be cultural. Private employers in the capital, many of which initially dismissed the scheme, have begun to copy it. Recruitment agencies report that flexible schedules now rank alongside salary as the most requested benefit among applicants in their thirties.

## The cost question

The scheme compresses a standard week into four longer days rather than cutting hours, so the direct cost has been modest. Managers say the harder adjustment has been scheduling: some departments have struggled to keep counters staffed on Fridays.

For the employees who have made the switch, the verdict is clear. In the latest survey, fewer than two per cent said they wanted to return to a five-day week.
