Local elections rarely excite anyone except the candidates. This year was different. Turnout reached its highest level in three decades, and the biggest increase came from a group that has long been written off as unreachable: voters under 30.

Exit polling suggests that 44 per cent of eligible under-30s voted, up from 33 per cent at the last comparable elections. In university towns and fast-growing commuter suburbs, the effect was enough to change control of at least six councils.

## Three reasons

**Housing.** In almost every area where youth turnout surged, local housing plans were a central issue. Candidates who campaigned on building more homes, particularly for rent, did markedly better.

**Registration.** Changes that allow people to register to vote when they apply for a driving licence or student loan added an estimated 900,000 younger voters to the rolls.

**Organisation.** Groups that began as tenant unions and campaign WhatsApp chats turned themselves into effective get-out-the-vote operations, often with no party affiliation.

## Will it last?

Previous surges in youth participation have faded quickly. The difference this time, according to Dr Ben Hartley, a political scientist who studies turnout, is that the issue driving it is local and concrete. "Nobody's rent went down because of a slogan," he said. "If these councils deliver homes, people will remember. If they don't, they'll remember that too."
