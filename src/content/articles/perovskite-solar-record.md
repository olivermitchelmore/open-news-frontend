Researchers have built a solar cell that converts more than 34 per cent of the sunlight that hits it into electricity, a new record for a device that could be manufactured at scale.

The cell layers a thin film of perovskite, a class of crystal that can be printed cheaply, on top of a conventional silicon cell. The two materials absorb different parts of the solar spectrum, allowing the combined device to capture more energy than either could alone.

Standard silicon panels on rooftops today typically convert around 20 to 22 per cent of sunlight.

## The durability problem

Perovskites have a well-known weakness: they degrade when exposed to heat, moisture and ultraviolet light. The team says its new cell retained 95 per cent of its performance after 1,000 hours of testing under harsh conditions, a significant improvement but still well short of the 25 years expected of commercial panels.

"Efficiency records are exciting, but the market will judge us on how long these things last on a roof," said Dr Mei Lin Tan, who led the work. Several manufacturers are already building pilot production lines, and the first tandem panels are expected to go on sale within two years.
