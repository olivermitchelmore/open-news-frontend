Every search, stream and chatbot reply runs on a server somewhere, and those servers need power. Lots of it. Global data centre electricity use has climbed from around 190 terawatt-hours a decade ago to an estimated 520 this year, roughly the annual consumption of a mid-sized European country.

Most of that growth has come in the past three years, driven by the chips used to train and run artificial intelligence models, which draw far more power than conventional servers.

## Where the power comes from

The largest operators have signed a record volume of long-term contracts with wind and solar farms this year, and several have agreed deals to buy power from new nuclear plants. Taken together, clean sources now account for around three-quarters of the electricity contracted for new facilities.

The catch is timing. Wind and solar farms take years to connect to the grid, and data centres are being built faster than that. In the meantime, many are running on gas, either from the grid or from turbines installed on site.

## The efficiency question

There is some good news. The amount of computing done per unit of electricity continues to improve, and newer facilities use far less energy for cooling than older ones. The best-performing sites now spend less than ten per cent of their power on anything other than the servers themselves.

But efficiency gains have so far been overwhelmed by growth. "Every time we make computing cheaper, we find more things to do with it," said Dr Farah Siddiqui, who studies energy systems at a university in London. "That is great for innovation. It is harder for the grid."
