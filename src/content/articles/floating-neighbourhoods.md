On a disused dockside at the edge of the delta, cranes are lowering concrete hulls into the water. Each will carry a three-storey terrace of homes, moored to steel piles that let the whole street rise by up to four metres when the river floods.

The project, the largest floating neighbourhood yet attempted, is a departure for a region that has spent centuries building ever-higher dykes. "Walls work until they don't," said Marleen Bakker, the city's chief planner. "We wanted to design a place that stays dry because it moves, not because it resists."

## Built to bend

The 400 homes are connected by hinged walkways and flexible utility lines that carry water, power and sewage. Rainwater is collected on green roofs, and heat pumps draw warmth from the river itself. Early modelling suggests the district will use a third less energy than a comparable development on land.

Critics point to the cost, roughly 15 per cent higher per home than conventional construction, and question whether insurers will cover properties that are, technically, boats. The city has negotiated a pooled insurance scheme with three providers, but premiums have not yet been published.

## A template for others

Delegations from Jakarta, Lagos and Dhaka have visited the site this year. Bakker is careful not to oversell it. "This is not the answer for a city of 20 million," she said. "But it is one answer, and we are going to learn a great deal by living in it."

Residents are due to move in next April. More than 3,000 households applied for the first 120 homes offered for social rent.
