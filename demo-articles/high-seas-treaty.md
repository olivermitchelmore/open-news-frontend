Forty coastal and island nations signed an agreement in Lisbon on Thursday to place almost a third of the high seas under some form of protection by the end of the decade, the most ambitious conservation deal ever struck for waters beyond national borders.

The pact commits signatories to create a network of marine reserves across the Atlantic, Indian and South Pacific oceans, with bottom trawling, deep-sea mining and industrial fishing banned or strictly limited inside them. It will come into force once ratified by 30 parliaments, a threshold negotiators expect to cross within 18 months.

"For most of history the high seas have been treated as a place where anything goes," said Inês Carvalho, the Portuguese diplomat who chaired the final round of talks. "Today we have agreed that they are a place where some things do not."

## Who pays for the patrols

The hardest questions were left until the last night. Smaller island states, whose exclusive economic zones border many of the proposed reserves, argued that they would bear the cost of patrolling waters they do not own. The final text creates a shared enforcement fund, financed by a levy on licensed fishing fleets, and allows satellite tracking data to be used as evidence in disputes.

> We have the satellites, we have the data. What we have never had is the legal right to act on what we see.
>
> Tavita Leota, fisheries minister, Samoa

Conservation groups welcomed the deal but warned that several of the world's largest fishing nations had not signed. Without them, one analyst said, the reserves risk becoming "lines on a map that the biggest boats simply sail around".

## What happens next

Scientific panels will spend the next year proposing exact boundaries, drawing on data about migratory routes, spawning grounds and the location of deep-sea coral. The first reserves could be formally designated in 2028.

For the fishing communities of the Azores, where the talks were first proposed, the agreement is a cautious bet on the future. "Our grandfathers fished these waters without limits," said one skipper at the signing ceremony. "We would like our grandchildren to be able to fish them at all."
