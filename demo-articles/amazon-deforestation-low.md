Deforestation in the Amazon fell to its lowest level in ten years over the twelve months to August, according to satellite monitoring data published on Wednesday, a sign that tougher enforcement is beginning to slow the destruction of the world's largest rainforest.

Around 5,100 square kilometres of forest were cleared across the basin, a fall of 31 per cent on the previous year. The sharpest reductions were in areas where environmental agencies have focused raids on illegal logging camps and where banks have been required to check the land records of cattle ranchers before lending.

"This is what happens when you follow the money," said Rafael Mendes, a researcher at a monitoring institute in Belém. "The chainsaw is the last step. The first step is someone deciding that clearing land will pay."

## Fragile gains

Scientists warn that the gains are fragile. Fires, which are not always captured in clearing figures, remained high during an unusually dry season, and degradation, where forest is thinned rather than removed entirely, is harder to measure.

Indigenous territories, which cover about a quarter of the basin, again recorded far lower rates of loss than surrounding land. Campaigners said the figures strengthened the case for formally recognising dozens of territories whose legal status is still pending.
