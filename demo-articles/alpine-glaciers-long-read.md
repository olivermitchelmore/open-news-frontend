At four in the morning, the head torch beams of the survey team bob up the moraine like fireflies. By the time the sun clears the ridge, they are standing on the tongue of the glacier, drilling stakes into ice that will, by the end of the season, be a metre and a half thinner than it was in spring.

Anna Keller has measured this glacier every September for 22 years. She remembers when the survey took a full day. Now the ice ends so far up the valley that the team is back at the hut by lunch.

"People ask me if I am sad," she says, winding a measuring tape. "I am past sad. I am busy. There is a lot to write down before it goes."

## Blankets on the ice

Further down the valley, a ski resort has spent the summer covering the upper reaches of its glacier with white fleece. The fabric reflects sunlight and slows melting by as much as 60 per cent where it is laid. It is expensive, labour-intensive and, the resort's managers concede, only ever going to protect a tiny fraction of the ice.

> We are not saving the glacier. We are saving a ski run, for a few more years. I think it is important to be honest about that.
>
> Matteo Rossi, resort operations manager

Other schemes are more ambitious. In one valley, engineers are testing whether meltwater can be pumped back up the mountain and sprayed as artificial snow in winter, thickening the ice before summer returns. Early results are promising but the energy costs are high.

## A different kind of water

The loss of the glaciers matters far beyond the ski slopes. Alpine ice feeds rivers that supply drinking water, irrigation and hydroelectric power to millions of people across Europe. In hot, dry summers the glaciers act as a reservoir, releasing water just when it is needed most.

As they shrink, that buffer disappears. Hydrologists expect summer river flows in parts of the Rhône and Po basins to fall sharply within a generation, with knock-on effects for farming and power generation.

## What the villages remember

In the villages, the retreat is measured in stories. An old woman points to a chapel built, she says, to pray for the glacier to stop advancing. Now her grandson guides tourists to see where the ice used to be.

Keller finishes her measurements and looks up at the ridge. "My daughter asked me what I will do when there is nothing left to measure," she says. "I told her I will measure the rock. Someone should record what is left behind."
